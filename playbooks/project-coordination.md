# Playbook: Ongoing Project Coordination and Status Reporting

## WHY

Client onboarding establishes scope and expectations on day one, but a real engagement runs over weeks or months, during which priorities shift, unexpected technical findings emerge, and clients reasonably want to know what is actually happening without asking. A team that only communicates at the start and the end of an engagement, with silence in between, creates genuine anxiety even when the underlying work is going well — and makes any eventual scope or timeline change feel sudden rather than expected.

## WHAT This Playbook Delivers

A consistent rhythm of honest status communication throughout an engagement, a clear process for handling new requests or scope changes that inevitably arise mid-project, and a prioritized view of current work that both the team and the client can genuinely reference, rather than status living only in someone's head or a chat history nobody reviews.

## Prerequisites

A completed client onboarding (per that playbook) establishing initial scope, constraints, and success criteria to measure ongoing progress against.

## Steps

1. Establish a regular status reporting rhythm appropriate to the engagement's actual pace — weekly for an active multi-week build, less frequent for a slower-moving engagement — and hold it consistently rather than only reporting when there is notably good news to share.

2. Structure each status update around what actually changed since the last one: what was completed, what is currently in progress, what is blocked and why, and what is planned next — avoiding vague summaries that could apply to any week.

3. Surface blockers and risks honestly and as early as they are recognized, not once they have become urgent — a client informed early has options; a client informed only once a deadline is already at risk does not.

4. When a new request arrives mid-engagement, explicitly evaluate it against the original agreed scope (per client onboarding) before simply adding it to the work — determine honestly whether it fits within existing scope, requires a scope change conversation, or should be logged for a future phase rather than the current one.

5. Maintain a single, current, prioritized view of active and upcoming work, visible to both the team and, where appropriate, the client — priority should be explicit and current, not something someone has to ask about or infer from recent conversation.

6. When priorities genuinely shift, whether from new client input or a technical discovery changing the plan, update the prioritized work view and communicate the change explicitly, rather than letting the actual work silently diverge from what was last communicated.

7. At natural milestones (not only the final one), summarize progress against the original success criteria established during onboarding, honestly noting where the engagement is ahead, on track, or behind — an honest "behind" with a real reason and a real plan forward maintains more trust than an optimistic status later revealed to be inaccurate.

## Guardrail Check

Never expand scope to accommodate a new mid-engagement request without an explicit conversation with the client about the resulting impact on timeline, budget, or existing priorities, per this framework's guardrail on client communication requiring human review before anything is sent. Never let a genuinely significant blocker or risk go uncommunicated while waiting for a "better time" to raise it — early, honest communication of a real problem is always better than a client discovering it independently or too late to respond.

## Reference Implementation

This is a foundational team-operating practice rather than a single traced incident, structured to operate continuously between the client onboarding playbook (engagement start) and eventual project completion, maintaining the same honesty and verification discipline established throughout this framework's other playbooks — applied here to communication and prioritization rather than technical work.
